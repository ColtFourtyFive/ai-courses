# Research Notes — Module 1: What an LLM Actually Is

## Concept check

- **Next-word prediction as the beginner framing: still accurate in 2026, keep it.** Confirmed across multiple 2025–2026 sources (CSET Georgetown explainer, multiple technical explainers). No need to soften or replace it.
- **New nuance worth a one-line caveat: "thinking"/reasoning models.** Teens will see visible "thinking" or "reasoning" bubbles in ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini apps today (these became mainstream through 2025). Research confirms: reasoning models are *still* next-token predictors — they just generate a longer hidden "scratchpad" of tokens (chain-of-thought) before the final answer, which improves the odds of a good next guess. Under the hood it's "the same statistical game," just looped longer with more compute. Recommend one short aside in Module 1 (or explicitly punt to Module 13): *"Some AI you'll see today shows its 'thinking' before answering — that's still next-word prediction, just doing more of it, step by step, before committing to a final answer."* This prevents a sharp teen from thinking "wait, isn't the reasoning model doing something different?" and losing trust in the core framing.
- **Memory: the "no memory unless the app adds it" framing needs a light update, not a rewrite.** Confirmed current: ChatGPT (and similarly Claude, Gemini) now ship built-in memory features (OpenAI's "saved memories" + "chat history," rolled out to free users mid-2025; further "dreaming"/auto-curation update). This is a *great* real example to cite, not a contradiction — it actually **proves** the Module 2 "model vs. app" point: memory is bolted on by the app/chat product, not a property of the raw model. Recommend Module 1 say something like: *"By default, a fresh conversation with the raw model starts from zero. Some chat apps now add a memory feature on top that saves facts about you across chats — but that's the app doing extra work, not the model itself remembering."* This also sets up Module 2 nicely — don't over-explain memory here, just flag it exists and defer mechanism to Module 2.
- **Context window sizes: numbers have moved up significantly, avoid citing a specific number.** As of 2025–2026, flagship models (OpenAI, Google, Anthropic) commonly advertise context windows around 1 million tokens, up from ~4K–8K in early 2023 — a roughly 100x jump. **Caveat worth knowing (probably not for the module, maybe Go Deeper):** independent 2025 benchmarks (e.g., Adobe's NoLiMa, ICML 2025) found models don't reliably *use* their full advertised window — accuracy often degrades well before the stated limit. Recommendation: Module 1 should NOT quote a specific token count (ages fast, and the "real" usable number is contested) — stick to the plain-English framing already drafted ("what's in front of it right now, the context window") and skip specific numbers. If a number is wanted for flavor, frame it as "some models can hold a small book's worth of text at once" rather than "X million tokens."
- **Tokens:** framing in the existing draft ("word-pieces," not letters/whole words) is still accurate and matches how OpenAI/Anthropic tokenizers work today (byte-pair-encoding style). No update needed.
- **Training vs. inference (frozen model):** still accurate and current. Worth noting for the owner: this is *also* getting slightly more complicated by some products now doing continual fine-tuning / feedback-based updates in the background — but that's a product/company-level detail, not something a beginner needs, and doesn't change the "frozen at the moment you're talking to it" framing. No change needed to Module 1 text.
- **Parameters/"large":** framing still accurate; no update needed.
- **Overall verdict: the existing Curriculum.md Module 1 draft is accurate and does not need factual correction — it needs one small addition (the reasoning-model aside) and one small nuance upgrade (memory, framed as proof of the app-vs-model split rather than an exception).**

## Real business example(s)

**Best pick — well-documented, legally decided, easy for a teen to follow: Air Canada's chatbot (2024).**
- What happened: Air Canada's website chatbot told a customer (Jake Moffatt) he could buy a full-price ticket after a family death and claim a bereavement discount retroactively within 90 days. That was false — Air Canada's real policy required requesting the discount *before* travel. Moffatt relied on the chatbot's confident (wrong) answer, then got denied the discount.
- Outcome: Canada's Civil Resolution Tribunal ruled Air Canada liable — ordered them to pay ~$812 CAD. The tribunal explicitly rejected Air Canada's argument that "the chatbot is a separate legal entity responsible for its own actions," ruling a company is responsible for everything on its own website, chatbot included.
- Why it's the best pick for this module: it's small-stakes enough to explain in two sentences, it's a real legal case with a real outcome (not vague reporting), and it cleanly demonstrates "fluent and confident ≠ true" in a business context a teen can immediately get (an airline, a discount, a chatbot that just made something up).
- Sources: [CBC News — "Air Canada found liable for chatbot's bad advice on bereavement rates"](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/british-columbia/air-canada-chatbot-lawsuit-1.7116416); [AI Incident Database, Report 3673](https://incidentdatabase.ai/reports/3673/) (community-maintained incident tracker, good corroborating source).

**Second candidate, more "grown-up"/current, use only if the owner wants a second, journalism-flavored example (e.g., for a later module on verification/professions):** multiple 2025–2026 documented cases of newsrooms publishing AI-hallucinated quotes:
- Ars Technica pulled an entire story in Feb 2026 after a reporter used AI to extract quotes from a blog post and the AI fabricated quotes that were published as real.
- A New York Times Canada bureau piece (flagged by a reader, corrected May 2026) reportedly included an AI-generated summary of a politician's (Pierre Poilievre) views presented as an actual quote.
- Wired and Business Insider both removed articles from a freelance byline in 2025 over suspected AI-fabricated reporting.
- **Recommend NOT using these as the primary Module 1 example** — they're more recent/less settled than Air Canada, involve some ambiguity about exactly what went wrong editorially (AI use vs. verification failure), and are less clean for a first-example moment. Air Canada is the cleaner, more legally-settled, easier-to-explain pick for Module 1. These journalism cases could be great for **Module 3 (What It's Great At, Where It Breaks)** or **Module 12 (Safety/Responsible Use)** instead, where more nuance is welcome — flagging for the owner as a possible reuse elsewhere in the course.
- Sources: [Press Gazette — "AI in journalism: Live tracker of scandals and mistakes"](https://pressgazette.co.uk/publishers/digital-journalism/ai-journalism-mistakes/); [Slashdot — Ars Technica AI reporter apology](https://news.slashdot.org/story/26/02/16/0139206/ars-technicas-ai-reporter-apologizes-for-mistakenly-publishing-fake-ai-generated-quotes). Flagged below as harder to independently verify firsthand (see Couldn't Verify).

As of late 2025, researchers had documented **200+ court cases** involving AI-generated fake legal citations, with the rate reportedly accelerating from ~2/week to 2–3/day. Named examples: a California attorney fined $10,000 after 21 of 23 case quotations in a brief were fabricated; three Morgan & Morgan lawyers sanctioned (Feb 2025) for AI-fabricated case citations. This is a great "this isn't a one-off, it's a pattern" stat if the module wants a punchier real-world-scale line, but the Air Canada story is still the better *narrative* example for a 13–16 audience.

## Interactive block

Brief calls for: run one prompt three times, changing a single word of the setup each time (mood / setting / audience), and watch how much the output shifts. Designed to work as copy-paste starter prompts in a simple embedded AI sandbox.

**Template A — mood swap.** Base prompt (paste once, then just swap the bracketed word and re-run):
> "Write a two-sentence text message from a student telling their friend they can't come to the party tonight. The student is feeling **[EXCITED]**."
- Run 1: `[EXCITED]` → Run 2: swap to `[GUILTY]` → Run 3: swap to `[ANNOYED]`.
- **What to notice:** word choice, punctuation/emoji, sentence length, even whether an apology appears — should visibly shift with just that one word, even though the "task" (write the text message) never changed.

**Template B — setting swap.** Base prompt:
> "Describe what it's like to walk into the school cafeteria at lunchtime, set in **[a normal Tuesday]**."
- Run 1: `[a normal Tuesday]` → Run 2: swap to `[the day after your team won states]` → Run 3: swap to `[the last day of school before summer]`.
- **What to notice:** the vocabulary, energy, and specific details invented all change based on one phrase — the model is reacting to the pattern the setting word implies, not "knowing" anything real about a cafeteria.

**Template C — audience swap.** Base prompt:
> "Explain why sleep matters for teenagers. Write it for **[a 7-year-old]**."
- Run 1: `[a 7-year-old]` → Run 2: swap to `[a skeptical teenager]` → Run 3: swap to `[a doctor writing a medical pamphlet]`.
- **What to notice:** vocabulary level, tone, sentence complexity, and even *which facts get included* change dramatically — same underlying "task," wildly different output, because the model is predicting what plausibly comes next *given* that one audience cue.

**Design notes for the writer/build:**
- Keep exactly one bracketed word/phrase swappable per template — that's the whole point (isolate the variable).
- Recommend the module literally bold or highlight the swappable word in the UI so it's unmissable in a simple embedded sandbox (no need for anything fancier than a text box + "run" button, 3x).
- Success criteria for the artifact (per Master-Build-Plan: "experiment log — 3 cases where changing one word flipped the output"): learner should be able to paste in the 3 outputs per template (or across templates) and write one sentence per case on what changed and why (tying back to "predicting what plausibly comes next given the words in front of it").
- Templates A/B/C give the writer three ready options — recommend picking ONE as the primary Try-It (not all three) to avoid padding, per AGENTS.md "don't pad" guardrail. Template A (mood swap via text message) is likely the most teen-native/fastest to grasp; Template C (audience swap) most directly demonstrates the "brilliant intern" analogy (an intern also changes how it explains something based on who's asking). Flagging both as strong candidates for the owner to pick between.
